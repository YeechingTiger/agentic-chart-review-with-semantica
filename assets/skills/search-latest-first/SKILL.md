# Entering late and reading upstream

A record is written forward by people who did not yet know how it turned out. It is EXPLAINED
backwards, by later authors who did. Those two facts pull in opposite directions, and every
other card in this slot moves forward or by document type. This one moves in time.

## Enter at the last document that summarises

Not the last document — the last one written to summarise the case rather than to record a
single event. Such a document restates the history in one place, in the vocabulary this site
actually uses, and it usually names the dates and the documents that matter. Reading it first
buys you the shape of the case and the local dialect for one read.

That is an entry point, not an answer. Take from it: which episodes exist, what this site calls
them, and which earlier documents it points at. Then go upstream to those.

## A summary is a report of an event, not the event

A later document saying that something was established on some date is a claim ABOUT a document.
Whether that claim can settle the field is the contract's decision, not this card's — and for
most fields it cannot, because the contract asks for the record that established the value, not
for a later author's recollection of it.

So treat a summary as the highest-value POINTER in the record and never as a witness unless the
contract says a statement of that kind establishes the field. A summary can also be wrong about
its own history, and it is frequently copied forward unchanged from an earlier summary that was
wrong first.

What you owe it is verification of what it REFERS TO — which episode, which subject, which
event — not necessarily a document written on the date it names. Some contracts explicitly let
a later authority state that something was true earlier, and require the earlier date. Where a
contract says that, demanding a contemporaneous document rejects evidence the contract admits.
Check the contract before you decide what corroboration means here.

## Let the task's time window exclude before terms do

The contract defines the period the answer is about. A document outside it cannot answer, however
well it matches your terms — and matching well is exactly the danger, because the same words
describe the same situation in a different episode. Bound the window first, then search inside
it. A search run without the window returns the right words from the wrong episode, and nothing
about the hit itself says so.

## When the task asks for the FIRST, the best document is the wrong one

The clearest, most complete, most quotable document is usually late, because clarity accumulates.
If the contract asks when something was first established, that document is evidence of the
opposite: everything it states so well was known by then, not first known then. Work back from it
to the earliest record the contract would accept — not to the one you would most like to quote.

Whether it accepts it may depend on a LATER document: contracts often admit an early, tentative
record only when something afterwards confirms what it was tentative about. So the earliest
candidate and its later confirmation are frequently one piece of evidence in two documents, and
stopping at the first of them is stopping halfway.

## What this cannot do

Entering late tells you nothing about anything no summary mentioned. Summaries record what their
author considered relevant, and a document nobody summarised is invisible to this route entirely
— which is a different blindness from the one a term-based search has, and not a smaller one. If
your answer claims something is absent, that claim rests on the documents this route never
reached, and `coverage-judgement` applies on top of this card.
